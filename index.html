Backend Data & Methodology
Every number in JourneyMate is sourced and traceable. This page documents the RAG architecture, cost model, ancillary revenue methodology, and NPS model.
Production RAG Architecture
In the prototype, context is pre-defined. In production, JourneyMate retrieves live data from three sources at the moment of disruption and passes it to the LLM as context.
RAG Pipeline — step by step
1
Disruption trigger
OAG / FlightAware API detects delay 90+ min, cancellation, or diversion. Event fires with flight number, reason code, new ETA, and affected PNR list.
↓
2
Profile retrieval — Source 1
PNR system API (Navitaire / Altea) retrieves traveller profile: tier, seat preference, meal choice, past ancillary history, contact channel. Read-only, sub-200ms.
↓
3
Inventory retrieval — Source 2
Real-time partner APIs: lounge availability (Priority Pass), hotel inventory (Amadeus), transfer availability, next flight (GDS feed). All retrieved simultaneously.
↓
4
Policy retrieval — Source 3 (RAG)
Vector similarity search in Pinecone DB against embedded DGCA policy corpus and airline entitlement rules. Returns top-k relevant chunks. Auto-updated when DGCA publishes new circulars.
↓
5
Generate and deliver
Profile + inventory + policy assembled into prompt. Claude Haiku for simple delays, Sonnet for complex cancellations. Delivered via WhatsApp/push/SMS in under 8 seconds from trigger.
↓
6
Feedback loop
Traveller accepts or dismisses each recommendation. Logged to segmentation model. JourneyMate gets smarter with every disruption handled.

Why RAG and not fine-tuning? DGCA policy changes frequently. Fine-tuning bakes knowledge at training time — it goes stale. RAG retrieves at inference time — always current. For policy-critical content like compensation entitlements, retrieval-grounded generation is non-negotiable. The LLM's job is language. The data pipeline's job is facts.

Experimentation plan · Falsifiable hypotheses · A/B design
A demo is an opinion.
An experiment is evidence.
Ten years of AI products teaches one thing: the graveyard is full of beautiful demos that were never falsifiable. So before we scale to 1.5 million disrupted passengers, every claim on the previous pages gets a chance to die — publicly, measurably, on a pre-registered threshold we commit to before seeing a single data point.
The operating doctrine — how we run AI experimentsHard-won rules
1 · Pre-register or it didn't happen
Thresholds are written down before the data arrives. Post-hoc goalposts are how AI teams lie to themselves — usually sincerely.
2 · Design the funeral before the launch
Every hypothesis carries its own kill decision. If nobody can say what failure looks like, it isn't an experiment — it's a rollout wearing a lab coat.
3 · Never experiment on people's rights
Legal entitlements are delivered identically in both arms, always. We A/B the experience, never the law. This line is non-negotiable and it is what makes the rest ethical.
4 · Instrument the failure, not just the win
Hallucination rate, action reversals, complaint-per-message — the metrics that can embarrass us are the ones on the dashboard. Vanity metrics are for the press release.
5 · Distrust week one
Novelty inflates everything. We read trends, not snapshots — a lift that fades by week four was never a lift, it was a honeymoon.
6 · The pilot buys the numbers, not the applause
The output of this pilot is a re-modelled CBA with measured — not assumed — automation and conversion rates. The business case earns its decimals here.

How we test each hypothesisSimple version
A simple 50/50 split works for most experiments. But three of our five questions need a different kind of test — so instead of forcing one method everywhere, we match the test to the question. Tap any row for the reasoning.
H1, H2 — split passengers into groups and compare
Standard A/B ▾
The straightforward approach works here — we just make sure every tier and disruption type is fairly represented in each group, not left to chance.
H3 — does trust today change behaviour later?
Track over time ▾
This isn't a same-day comparison — we follow the same passengers across weeks, from their disruption experience to their next trip-planning moment.
H4 — is the AI making things up?
Watch continuously ▾
Too important to wait 30 days to find out. We check this every week and can shut it down on day 6 if something's wrong — not on a fixed schedule.
H5 — does this only work where passengers have a choice of airline?
Compare across routes ▾
One route can't answer a question about different routes. This runs as a small side study comparing a few routes with more or less competition — we say so honestly rather than force one pilot to answer everything.

Five hypotheses — each one hunts for a way we could be fooling ourselvesTap any card
A weak hypothesis asks "did the metric go up?" A useful one asks "what's the ONE way we could be wrong, and how do we find out on purpose?" These five target the assumptions the whole business case actually rests on.
H1 · Is it the personalisation — or just the speed?
beats fast-generic by ≥8 NPS ▾
The riskiest assumption isn't "does JourneyMate help" — it's "does the expensive part (segmentation, RAG, personalisation) earn its keep, or would a fast generic message get 80% of the benefit for 20% of the engineering cost?" We run a hidden third arm: fast-but-generic. If personalisation only matches the generic-fast arm, then we've been paying for sophistication the passenger never felt.
☠ Kill decision: if fast-generic performs within noise of personalised, we strip the product back to speed alone for simple delays and reinvest the saved engineering effort in the cases where personalisation actually shows — this is the humbling hypothesis, and we designed it to be.
H2 · Do people actually trust it — or just tolerate it?
verify-call rate < 15% ▾
NPS surveys measure what people say. This measures what they do. If passengers genuinely trust an AI-delivered entitlement, then they act on it without calling a human to double-check it's real — today that verification-call rate on complex disruptions runs above 70%.
☠ Kill decision: if passengers keep calling to verify despite receiving the plan, the trust deficit is real regardless of what the survey says — we insert a lightweight human confirmation step before scaling automation further, and we say so publicly rather than declare victory on a satisfied-sounding survey.
H3 · Does trust earned in Recovery Mode pay for itself in Companion Mode?
measurable conversion lift ▾
This is the hypothesis that ties the whole roadmap together. Our zero-upsell rule in Recovery Mode is a real cost — we're leaving money on the table in the moment of maximum leverage. If that restraint is actually building trust rather than just being polite, then passengers who lived through a well-handled Recovery Mode disruption should convert HIGHER in Companion Mode later than passengers who never did.
☠ Kill decision: if there's no conversion difference between the two cohorts, our "trust flywheel" story in the roadmap is a narrative, not a mechanism — we keep the zero-upsell rule on ethical grounds regardless, but we stop presenting it as a monetisation strategy to the board.
H4 · Does grounding hold at the edges, or only on the easy average case?
edge-case error ≈ overall ▾
An overall hallucination rate of 0.3% can hide a nasty tail — averages flatter you. If our validator genuinely generalises, then the hardest 10% of cases — family plus overnight plus missed international connection plus stacked entitlements — should show an error rate close to the overall number, not a spike.
☠ Kill decision: if complex cases show materially higher error, we don't average our way past it — those specific patterns get routed to human-assisted generation only, permanently, while automation continues for the simple majority. We'd rather have an honest seam than a hidden failure mode.
H5 · Does recovery quality retain passengers — or only where they have a choice?
effect scales with route competition ▾
The LTV-protection line in our CBA assumes disruption handling changes loyalty. But on a route with only one carrier, a passenger has nowhere else to go regardless of how they're treated. If recovery quality is a genuine loyalty lever, then the 90-day rebooking lift should be visibly larger on competitive routes than on near-monopoly ones — not a flat effect everywhere.
☠ Kill decision: if the effect is flat regardless of competition, we're likely measuring something else — general goodwill, not switching-relevant loyalty — and we discount the LTV line in the CBA specifically for monopoly-heavy networks rather than let one aggregate number overstate the case everywhere.

Explainable AI — "why?" is a product feature, not a compliance checkboxXAI
Most teams bolt explainability on for the auditors. We built it for the passenger, the agent, and the regulator — three audiences, three explanations, one system of record.
AudienceThe question they askThe explanation they get
The passenger"Why am I being offered this?"Every message carries a tap-to-expand "why this": the DGCA paragraph behind each entitlement, and the preference signal behind each option ("aisle seat — your choice on 34 of your last 36 bookings"). Recommendations you can interrogate are recommendations you can trust.
The service agent"What did the AI already do, and why?"Escalations arrive with the full decision trace pre-loaded — retrieved facts, actions taken, authorisation tier applied. The human never starts from zero and never contradicts the machine blindly.
The regulator / auditor"Prove this decision was grounded"An immutable per-message audit record: which corpus version, which retrieved chunks, which model version, which validator checks passed. Any message from any day is reconstructable, claim by claim.
Even the segmentation explains itself: clusters are described by their driving features in plain language ("this passenger is Cluster 1 primarily due to flight frequency and lounge usage") — no black-box scores deciding how a human being gets treated during the worst hour of their trip.

A note to whoever builds the next one
The model is the least interesting part of an AI product — it will be swapped twice before your Series B. What compounds is everything around it: the evals that let you change anything safely, the retrieval that keeps you honest, the guardrails that let you sleep, and the trust you refused to spend on a quick upsell.

Ship the boring scaffolding first. Earn autonomy one reversible action at a time. Write your failure thresholds before your press release. And when the demo gets applause, remember: applause is not evidence.

Each phase unlocks only when the previous phase's outcome metric is met. Responsible AI is not a compliance slide at the end — every guardrail below is a functional requirement in the LangFlow pipeline.
The JourneyMate Autonomy FrameworkCapability-gated, not calendar-gated
Calendar dates can't promise how an AI system will behave — but they can absolutely govern how much runway we're willing to spend finding out. So every level below carries two different things: a gate that decides whether we advance, and a budget window that decides how long we keep trying before we stop and re-scope. The gate is about truth. The clock is about capital discipline. Neither one alone is enough.

L1
Advisory recovery — the AI proposes, a tap confirms
Every rebooking, lounge pass, or voucher is drafted by the AI and confirmed by the passenger in one tap. Nothing executes on its own yet. This is the entire pilot.
🔓 Gate to advance: H1-H4 clear their pre-registered thresholds; hallucination rate sustained under 0.5%
⏱ Budget: 8 weeks, ~$30K — a slice of the $175K dev total on the Vision page, spent specifically on pilot integration. If trending positive but not cleared → one 4-week extension, written reason required. If not trending → stop and re-scope, not drift
👤 Decision owner: Product Head + pilot airline sponsor, jointly — not a solo call
L2
Trusted recovery — free, reversible actions auto-execute
Lounge access and vouchers happen automatically — nothing to confirm. Anything that changes the journey (rebooking, seat) still gets a tap. Rolled out across every tier and route.
🔓 Gate to advance: reversal rate stays low in live production (not just pilot) for a sustained run — earned in the field, not the lab
⏱ Budget: 6 months from Phase 0 exit — the remaining $145K dev + $128K Year-1 ops from the Vision page CBA. Together with L1 this completes the $302K total above; no new number introduced here
👤 Decision owner: Product Head + CFO sign-off — this is where spend materially increases
L3
Companion autonomy — proactive, never during a disruption
The AI can now proactively suggest trip planning and cross-sell — but only at booking, pre-trip, and post-trip moments. It is architecturally incapable of doing this during a live disruption.
🔓 Gate to advance: H3 proven — passengers who lived through good Recovery Mode convert measurably higher, not just anecdotally
⏱ Budget: 6 months from Phase 1 exit, ~$54K — new incremental spend, not in the original CBA. Funded from Recovery Mode's own savings once proven, not a fresh capital ask
👤 Decision owner: Product Head + Commercial lead — first phase with a revenue line to defend
L4
Platform autonomy — one system, many airlines, configured not coded
Each airline sets its own autonomy budget and thresholds through configuration — the same evaluated system, different policy dials. No forked codebase per customer.
🔓 Gate to advance: unit economics proven on real numbers from airline one, eval harness proven portable to a second corpus
⏱ Budget: 8-10 months from Phase 2 exit, ~$84K per new airline onboarded — also self-funded from cash flow by this point, not new external capital
👤 Decision owner: CEO + Board — a second airline is a strategic bet, not a product decision
Gates are segment-level, not all-or-nothing: if Platinum clears at week 8 but Gold doesn't, we advance Platinum into Phase 1 and hold Gold for the extension window — we don't block a working segment because another segment needs more evidence. Partial success gets a partial decision, not a shrug.
Honest dependency: Phase 0 rests on one pilot airline partner. If they delay or walk, the timeline slips with them. Mitigation in progress: early conversations with a second carrier as backup, and the architecture is built airline-agnostic by design — switching partners costs weeks of re-integration, not months of rebuilding.
Rigour has a cost, and we're not pretending otherwise: every week we wait for a gate is a week a competitor could move first. We chose the shortest budget window that still tells signal from noise — not the safest one. If someone ships a weaker version faster and wins the deal, that's a real risk we're accepting consciously, not one we're ignoring.
Why this framework, not a Gantt chart: a date-based roadmap quietly promises a specific model will behave a specific way by a specific month — a promise nobody can actually make about AI systems. A capability-gated ladder promises something we control instead: the rigour of the gate itself. The budget windows keep us honest about burn — every day costs money, so "we'll wait until it's ready" is not a license to drift. Every window ends in a decision, not a shrug.

How JourneyMate knows what to say

Every personalised message JourneyMate generates is grounded in real retrieved data — not hallucinated. This page explains the full RAG pipeline: from live data ingestion to the final AI-generated response delivered to the traveller.

Core design principle
Facts from retrieval. Language from the model.
RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation. Instead of relying on what the AI model was trained on (which goes stale), RAG retrieves fresh, relevant data at the moment of generation and passes it as context to the LLM. The model then generates language — not facts.

🚫
Why not fine-tuning?
DGCA policy changes frequently. Fine-tuning bakes knowledge into the model at training time — it goes stale. A fine-tuned model might cite outdated compensation rules and create legal liability.
✓
Why RAG works here
Policy corpus in Pinecone is updated whenever DGCA publishes new circulars. Every generation retrieves the current rule — not a training-time snapshot. Always accurate, always current.
🔒
Hallucination prevention
The LLM is explicitly instructed to generate language only from retrieved context. If entitlement data is not in the retrieved chunks, the model does not invent it — it says what it knows.

Step 3 — Vector database
Pinecone — how the policy corpus works
The DGCA policy corpus is pre-processed, chunked, and embedded into Pinecone before any disruption event. At retrieval time, the disruption context is embedded and the most semantically similar policy chunks are returned in milliseconds.
📦 Pinecone vector database — JourneyMate policy index
Document ingestion
DGCA circulars, airline fair practice codes, and tier entitlement rules are loaded as PDF/text documents into LangFlow's document loader node.
Chunking strategy
Documents are chunked at 512 tokens with 50-token overlap. Each chunk represents one coherent policy rule — not split mid-sentence.
Embedding model
text-embedding-3-small (OpenAI) converts each chunk to a 1536-dimension vector. Same model used at retrieval time for consistency.
Retrieval query
At disruption, the query "passenger entitlements for [disruption type] on [route] for [tier]" is embedded and top-5 nearest chunks are returned by cosine similarity.
Auto-update pipeline
A scheduled LangFlow workflow monitors DGCA's official portal weekly. New circulars trigger automatic re-ingestion, re-chunking, and re-embedding.
Namespace separation
Policy corpus, traveller profiles, and inventory data are stored in separate Pinecone namespaces. No cross-contamination between retrieval contexts.
Step 4 — LLM routing
Two model tiers, routed by severity — vendor is a config, not a commitment
Not every disruption needs the same reasoning depth. JourneyMate routes to the appropriate model tier based on disruption complexity — optimising speed, cost, and quality simultaneously. The architecture is model-agnostic: every model sits behind one abstraction layer, so swapping vendors is a configuration change gated by our evaluation suite — never a rebuild. Claude Haiku and Sonnet are the current eval-selected champions; Gemini Flash or GPT-class models are drop-in challengers.

Architecture — the decisions, not the code
Seven deliberate choices a production system demands
Each of these was a fork in the road. What matters isn't the technology name — it's why we chose, and what it protects.
Retrieve, don't memorise
Policy facts are looked up fresh at every generation, never baked into the model. DGCA changes a rule → we update one document, and every message is correct the same day. No stale-knowledge legal risk.
The AI proposes, our code decides
The AI can trigger real actions — rebooking, lounge, vouchers — but only from a fixed menu, and only within an authorisation tier we defined: free reversible actions run automatically, journey changes need one passenger tap, refunds always reach a human. Even a tricked AI cannot exceed its permissions.
Validate before send
Every factual claim in a draft — every rupee amount, every entitlement — is checked against its retrieved source before delivery. Unsupported claim → message blocked, safe fallback sent. Target: under 0.5% error, human-audited weekly, automatic rollback on breach.
Share the facts, personalise the message
One broken flight = 178 passengers sharing identical flight facts and policy rules. We fetch and cache those once; each passenger's message is still composed individually from their own profile. Result: ~90% lower AI cost — the ingredients are shared, the dish never is.
Right-size the model
80% of disruptions are simple → fast, cheap model in under a second. The complex 20% — families, overnights, missed international connections — route to the stronger model. A severity classifier makes the call. And the models themselves are swappable configuration behind one interface — today's choice won the evals; any model that beats it takes its place. No vendor lock-in by design.
Nothing ships on vibes
Every prompt, model, or policy-corpus change passes a four-level evaluation gate — from plain automated checks to human-calibrated review — then runs invisibly in parallel with the live version for a week before touching a single passenger.
Silence is never a mode
Every pipeline stage has a fallback: APIs fail → the message omits that source; the AI fails → a plain entitlement template goes out; anything sensitive → a human, with context pre-loaded. The passenger always hears something true, within seconds.
The through-line: the AI is never trusted with anything deterministic rules can enforce. Retrieval owns the facts, policy code owns the permissions, evals own the quality — the AI owns only the language. One principle, applied everywhere.
